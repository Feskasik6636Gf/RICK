You'd wake up in the safest world in human history. And you'd have exactly one generation to stop it from ending.

It's a Tuesday, 6:02 a.m., and your phone is vibrating off the nightstand.

Fourteen missed calls. A message from your dad. It says, "Don't panic."

That's the single most panic-inducing sentence a parent can send.

You open the news. Every anchor on every channel is a woman. Every guest. Every reporter outside every building. Not a single man on screen, in the background, or in the crowd behind them. A reporter is holding a microphone with a shaking hand. She says, "We're getting reports from every country. Overnight, every person on Earth has become female."

She pauses. She looks down at her own hands.

"Including me."

You run to the mirror.

You don't need to see yourself. You already know. Because the world has changed overnight, and nobody had a vote.

Your phone rings. Dad.

You answer. A voice you've never heard says, "Hey, kiddo."

It's higher. It's softer. But the way she laughs, the way she says kiddo, the way she's already making a joke because she's terrified? That's all him.

"I'm still your dad," she says. "I just have better hair and worse knees."

You laugh. You cry. You do both at once.

And that's the strange part of this story. Nobody's skills changed. Nobody's memories changed. Nobody forgot how to fly a plane, perform surgery, or fix a bridge. The pilots in the air land safely. The surgeons keep operating. The trains keep running. Every single person on Earth is exactly who they were the night before.

Just not the same body.

And here's where it gets weird.

By the end of the first week, something quiet settles over the world. Police scanners go calm. Not empty. People are still people. There are still arguments, thefts, and broken windows. But the kind of violence that fills the news every night begins to fade. Researchers say that men commit about ninety percent of the world's murders. Before that Tuesday, about ninety-three of every hundred prisoners on Earth were men. Now every person in every prison is a woman, and the guards say it's the quietest week of their careers.

Cities get calmer. Streets feel different. Women who used to walk home with their keys between their fingers stop doing it.

Some of it is almost funny. The line for every women's bathroom disappears. Clothing stores run out of one size and have too much of another. Sports leagues cancel their seasons because nobody knows which records to keep. A man who used to be a bodybuilder posts a video of himself trying to open a jar. The jar wins. Doctors point out another change, too. Women live about five years longer than men on average, and some people start joking that the world just got a lot older and wiser.

And for six days, everyone agrees. It's the best thing that's ever happened.

On the seventh day, you go back to work.

You're a lab technician at a fertility clinic. Nights, mostly. The clinic is on the fourth floor of an old hospital, and it has one very important room. A cold, humming, white-lit room full of silver tanks, each one the size of a trash can, each one filled with liquid nitrogen, each one holding thousands of tiny frozen vials at minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees.

Sperm. Eggs. Embryos. The future of a hundred thousand families.

You never thought about it much. It's your job. You check the temperature, refill the tanks, and sign the log.

Tonight, a woman is waiting at the door.

Her name is Dr. Katarina Voss. She's a geneticist from the university across the river, and she's holding a folder with her hands clenched around it.

"I need to show you something," she says. "And then I need your help."

She puts a sheet of paper in front of you. A single graph. A line goes straight down.

"Every human being on Earth now has two X chromosomes," she says. "Every person who is able to have a child can no longer have a child with anyone. Not naturally. Not ever. Do you understand what that means?"

You do. You just haven't let yourself say it.

"No more babies."

"No new babies. The youngest generation on Earth is the last one, unless we find a way. And here's the thing." She taps the graph. "Fertility doesn't last. The average woman reaches menopause around fifty-one. Every year, the number of women who can carry a child falls. In about forty years, there won't be any left."

You stare at the page.

"So we have forty years."

"We have less," she says. "Because we won't have forty healthy years. Panic, grief, wars over what's left, the collapse of everything. Unless we do something now."

She looks at the silver tanks behind you.

"We start here."

Remember those tanks. One of them is going to change everything.

Quick pause. If you woke up tomorrow in this world, what would you miss most? Drop it below. The best comment is next week's what-if.

For the next three weeks, you and Dr. Voss don't sleep. You count every vial in every freezer on the floor. You find out how much is left in the entire hospital system. Then the city. Then the country.

It's not enough. Not nearly.

But it's a start. And it's real. Babies have been born from sperm frozen for more than thirty years. In 2022, a baby girl was born from an embryo that had been frozen since 1992. She started out older than the woman who carried her. Science already knows how to wait.

The problem isn't the science. The problem is the supply.

And there's a bigger problem. A man in a lab coat shows up on the news the following Monday. He used to be a man. Now he's Dr. Henrietta Caldwell, and she says something that makes your stomach drop.

"Within a few months, every public and private tank on Earth will start to fail."

Because those tanks need liquid nitrogen. Refilled every few weeks. And liquid nitrogen is made in a small number of plants, shipped by a small number of trucks, driven by a small number of people. And a lot of those people have quit.

They're grieving. They're scared. They've decided it's pointless.

Dr. Voss reads the same news, and she turns white. "How long does the clinic have?"

You check the gauge. You count on your fingers.

"Nine days."

That night, you stay in the freezer room until the sun comes up. You tell yourself you're just watching the numbers. But what you're actually doing is making a promise to a room full of strangers who haven't been born yet.

On day six, the supplier calls. There's a shipment, but the driver won't leave her house. There are riots in the next county. The roads are blocked. The nitrogen plant is two hundred miles away, and nobody will go.

So you do.

You borrow a delivery truck from a florist. You strap two empty tanks in the back. And you drive.

It's the longest road trip of your life. The highways are empty. The gas stations are dark. In one town, you pass a long line of women outside a church, praying quietly. In another, you pass a billboard that used to say "Dad's Day Sale." Someone has spray-painted a single word over the top. "Why?"

Halfway there, your truck dies.

You get out. You stare at the engine. You've never fixed anything in your life.

Then a voice says, "You're holding the hood wrong."

A very large woman in grease-stained overalls is standing at the side of the road with a toolbox. Her name is Roz. She was a long-haul trucker before that Tuesday. She was six foot four. Now she's five foot nine, and she's still furious about it.

"I used to bench two-fifty," she says. "Now I can barely lift the tailgate."

"I need to get to the nitrogen plant."

"Why?"

You tell her. All of it. The tanks, the clock, the forty years.

She doesn't say a word for a full minute. She just wipes her hands on a rag. Then she slams the hood and says, "Move over."

She drives the rest of the way. Fourteen hours straight. She curses every pothole. She's never been prouder of anything in her life.

At the plant, there's one tired worker left. She hands you a hose and says, "Take what you want. I've been here alone for two weeks. I didn't know why I kept showing up."

You fill both tanks. It takes three hours. And when you finally turn off the valve, the three of you stand in the cold light of the loading dock, steam rising off the metal, and nobody says anything.

On the ride home, Roz says, "Think it'll work?"

"I don't know."

"Good answer."

You make it back with six hours to spare.

Dr. Voss is waiting at the door, crying. She hugs you so hard your ribs hurt. Behind her, the freezer room hums. Every tank is alive.

Remember Roz. You'll see her again.

That night, while you refill the tanks one by one, you reach the last one in the corner. Tank nine. It's the oldest in the room, with a cracked label and a faded sticker you've never paid attention to. Inside, you find the oldest vials in the clinic. Most of them are from the nineties.

One of them has a name on the label.

It's your dad's.

You stare at it for a long time. You don't understand. You've never seen it before. There's a date. 1999. And below that, in a handwriting you've known your entire life, five words.

"For my kid. In case."

You call him. It rings once. "Hey, kiddo."

"Dad. Why is your name in tank nine?"

There's a long silence. When he speaks again, his voice is smaller.

"I was thirty-one. They told me the treatment could make it impossible. I'd just gotten you. You were two. And I thought, what if you want a brother? Or a sister? What if I couldn't give you one?" She breathes. "I never told you. I didn't want you to think I was sad."

You sit on the floor of the freezer room. You hold the vial against your chest. It's so cold it hurts.

"You froze a sibling for me."

"I froze a maybe."

Eleven weeks later, a scientist with a steady hand and a very good team does the thing nobody thought could happen. Dr. Voss leads it. Her team is also chasing a second path. In 2018, researchers in China made healthy mice with two mothers. It took years of tricky science, and it has never been done in humans. But the vials in tank nine are the path that works now.

You hold her hand when the call comes.

The volunteer is a woman in her forties. A nurse. She raised her hand at a public meeting in a small town the day after the news came out. Nobody had asked. She just stood up and said, "I'll do it."

Nine months later, on a Tuesday, at 3:12 in the morning, a baby is born.

The delivery room goes silent. Every doctor, every nurse, and every volunteer stares at the tiny, wet, perfect little face. Dr. Voss checks the chart twice. And then, in the quietest voice anyone has ever heard her use, she says, "It's a boy."

Nobody in the building has seen one in nearly a year.

Outside the hospital, a crowd has gathered. Thousands of women, standing in the dark, holding candles. Roz is at the front. She is crying openly. And when the news spreads through the crowd, it doesn't start with a cheer. It starts with silence. A deep, trembling, holy kind of quiet.

Then someone starts to clap.

Your dad is beside you. He holds the baby for the first time. She looks down at that small, red, crumpled face, and the biggest, softest smile you've ever seen spreads across her face.

"Hi," she whispers. "I'm your dad."

The baby opens his eyes.

Not bad for a kid who just wanted a quiet Tuesday.

You lost the world you knew. You nearly lost the future. You spent your nights counting vials, and your days driving through a country that had stopped believing in tomorrow. And you learned that survival isn't one big miracle. It's a thousand small people who keep showing up.

So tell me, if the whole world woke up one morning as women, would you want to go back? Or would you keep what you had, and fight for what was left?
