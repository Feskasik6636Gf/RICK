You'd end half the misunderstandings on Earth before lunch. And by the end of the week, you'd be the only thing standing between the world and a war that nobody could blame on a bad translation.

It's 8:59 a.m. at the United Nations in New York, and you're sitting in a glass booth the size of a closet.

You're an interpreter. One of the best in the world. For twenty years, you've sat in a tiny soundproof box with headphones on, and you've turned angry speeches into words that governments can survive. Nobody knows your name. That's the job. If you do it right, nobody ever knows you were there.

Below you, in the great hall, two ambassadors are shouting at each other across a table. They come from neighbouring countries that have been circling a border for decades. One speaks in his language. The other, in hers. And between them, there's you.

The first ambassador leans in and says something that, translated exactly, means "your people are liars and thieves."

You say, in your calm voice, "My colleague expresses serious concern about the accuracy of recent claims."

That's the secret of your job. Interpreters don't just translate. They soften. They sand off the sharp edges. Everyone in the room knows it, and nobody ever says it out loud. A lot of peace in the world is just very polite lying done by people in headphones.

You check the clock. 8:59. Interpreters work in shifts of about thirty minutes, because the brain can't keep up for longer. You're about to hand off to your partner.

Then the clock turns to nine.

And every language on Earth disappears.

Not the people. Not the voices. Just the languages. For about one second, the whole planet goes quiet. Eight billion people open their mouths to say something, and stop. Because every word they know is gone, and a new word is already there.

One language. The same one. Everywhere. For everyone. Nobody learned it. Nobody chose it. It's just in your head, in every head, as if it was always there.

In the hall below, the first ambassador finishes his sentence. The second one hears it. The headphones in front of her are still dangling around her neck. She doesn't need them.

She stands up so fast her chair hits the floor. "How dare you."

And you realize something terrible.

She heard every word.

Not your soft version. Not "serious concern." The real thing. The raw thing. The one you spent twenty years filtering out.

The hall erupts. Your partner stares at you through the glass. You pull off your headset. It's useless now. You're useless now.

Welcome to the first day of a world with one language.

At first, it's wonderful.

In Tokyo, a tourist asks a taxi driver where to find a good ramen shop. The driver laughs, takes him home, and feeds him dinner. In Cairo, a shopkeeper and a visitor from Brazil spend three hours talking about football, family, and bad knees. In Lagos, a doctor who has never been able to understand a frightened patient from a nearby village finally hears the one thing that matters. "It hurts here."

Emergency lines go quiet because every call is answered. Courtrooms lose their interpreters, and people can tell their own stories for the first time. Refugees stop being strangers at the border and become people with names. The whole world has been waiting for this its entire life. By noon, the news has one headline. It says, "We can finally talk."

Remember those words. They're about to turn into a joke.

Not everyone is celebrating, though. A million people wake up with no job. Interpreters, translators, language teachers, subtitle writers, tour guides who speak six languages. An entire industry worth tens of billions of dollars is gone before breakfast. Schools cancel every foreign language class. A girl in Madrid cries because she spent six years learning French, and now French doesn't exist. Movies lose their dubbing. Songs lose their lyrics. A fan in Brazil holds up a poster of her favorite K-pop band, and for the first time in her life, she understands every word. She's thrilled. Then she realizes the lyrics are only okay. The magic was in the not knowing.

By the afternoon, your phone is exploding. Your boss. The Secretary General's office. Two embassies. One desperate voice you've never heard. They all say the same thing. "We need you."

You say, "For what? Nobody needs an interpreter."

"No," says the voice. "We need the person who knows how to say things."

You walk into the great hall. The ambassadors have been separated into two rooms, but their delegations are screaming through the walls. Something has happened in the last four hours. A border guard heard a general say something about his village on live television, and for the first time in his life, he didn't need a headline to tell him what it meant. He understood every word. And he was furious.

Because here's the part nobody saw coming. For centuries, humans have been using language barriers as a kind of insurance. If something nasty was said, there was always a way out. "It was a mistranslation." "He didn't mean it like that." "That's not what the word means in our language."

Now there's no way out. Every insult lands exactly as it was thrown. Every threat sounds exactly as threatening as it was meant to be. And every person finds out, all at once, exactly what their neighbour thinks of them.

It turns out, the problem was never only that people couldn't understand each other. It was that some of them could.

By evening, the two ambassadors are on opposite sides of the building, refusing to speak. A third country sends a warning. A fourth moves ships. It's the oldest story in the world. Everyone has the information. Nobody has the grace.

So you do the one thing you've done your whole career.

You sit between them.

You don't translate. You can't. They already understand each other perfectly. Instead, you do something harder. You stand in front of one ambassador, and you say, "She didn't call your people liars. She called your report incomplete. That's a fact she can prove, and you can fix."

The ambassador says, "That's not what she said."

You say, "It's what she meant."

And that's when you finally understand your whole career. You were never just turning words into other words. You were turning anger into something people could survive.

Remember that. It's the most important thing you'll learn all week.

For three days, you walk from room to room, doing the same thing. Every language on Earth is gone, and you're still the only translator that matters. Not between tongues. Between hearts.

On the fourth day, you finally get a chance to go home.

You take the train. The subway is quiet. Everybody is looking at everybody, trying to figure out what to say, now that anyone can say anything. A teenager sits down next to a man in a suit and says, "Hey, what's your favorite song?" The man looks at him like he's been slapped. Then he smiles. And a woman across the aisle says, "Mine's the one about the sea."

The whole car starts to sing.

You get home at midnight. And your grandmother is sitting at the kitchen table.

Her name is Salma, and everyone calls her Teta. She came to this country at twenty, with one suitcase and a recipe book written in a language almost nobody here could read. For fifty years, she told stories in that language. She sang songs in it. She cursed in it, loudly, whenever you left the stove on.

You sit down. She's staring at a cup of tea.

"Teta?"

She looks up. She speaks. And for the first time in your life, her words are perfectly clear. No accent, no gaps, no stops where you used to fill in the meaning yourself. You can understand every sentence.

And that's the problem.

"I can't remember the joke," she says.

"What joke?"

"The one my mother told me. About the donkey and the well." She stares at her hands. "I know the story. I can tell you all of it. But it isn't funny anymore. It was only funny in the old words. In the old words, the donkey's name sounded like a sneeze. Now it's just a donkey."

She tries to sing you the lullaby she used to sing when you were little. She gets three lines in. She stops. The tune is right, but the words don't land. They don't rhyme anymore.

"It's like looking at a house," she says, "after somebody took the walls."

You put your arm around her. You don't have an answer. Nobody does.

Because that's the cost nobody counted. There were about seven thousand languages on Earth. Linguists say about half of them could have vanished by the end of this century anyway. But in one second, all of them went. Not just words. Ways of seeing.

In one Aboriginal language in Australia, there is no word for "left" or "right." Everything is north, south, east, and west. Speakers of that language always know exactly which way they're facing, even in a dark room. They don't have a compass. They have a sentence.

One language in the Amazon, spoken by a people called the Pirahã, is famous among linguists for having no exact number words. No "three." No "seven." Just "few" and "many." A whole way of thinking about the world, gone.

Russian speakers have two completely different words for light blue and dark blue, and studies show they can tell the shades apart faster than people who only have one. Your mother tongue doesn't just describe your world. It quietly shapes it.

And now all of it is silent.

At the end of the week, the news says the border crisis is over. The ambassadors shook hands. Nobody died. You get a medal you don't want. A reporter asks how you did it. You say, "I just told them the truth. Slowly."

Then you go home, and you sit with Teta, and you do something you've never done before.

You hum.

You hum the lullaby. Not the words, just the tune. She closes her eyes. She hums with you. And for a moment, neither of you needs a language at all.

Then your five-year-old daughter, Noor, walks into the room in her pajamas. She looks at the two of you and says something you've never heard.

"Why are you making the moon noise?"

Teta opens one eye. "The what?"

"The moon noise." Noor shrugs. "That's what it sounds like."

You laugh. Teta laughs. And Noor says it again, proud, with her whole chest. "Moon noise."

It isn't a word in the new language. She just invented it. And somehow, you both know exactly what it means.

That's when you notice something strange.

All week, the world has been trying to speak the same way. But the second people had a chance, they started changing it. A taxi driver in Mexico City started adding a little roll to the end of his words. A girl in Seoul invented a slang word for being late. A group of fishermen in Norway started using a nickname for the sea that nobody else had heard.

It takes about a thousand years for a language to split into new ones. That's how Latin became French, Spanish, and Italian. But slang? Teenagers need three days.

You look at Noor, rolling the new word around in her mouth. It's already hers. It will change in a month. It will change in a year. By the time she's your age, there will be a hundred new dialects, and by the time she has grandchildren, there will be thousands.

The planet didn't stay one language.

It never could.

Because language isn't something you're given. It's something you make, together, every day, one little moon noise at a time.

Not bad for an interpreter who just wanted to be heard.

You lost seven thousand languages, one lullaby, and a joke about a donkey that nobody will ever laugh at again. You gained a world where nobody can hide behind a bad translation. And you learned that understanding was never about words. It was about being willing to say the hard thing gently.

So tell me, if you could trade every language on Earth for perfect understanding, would you do it? And which word from your language would you miss the most?
