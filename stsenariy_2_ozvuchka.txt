You'll go to the funeral of every person you've ever loved. You'll watch the last person who remembers your face close their eyes. And it all starts with the best birthday party of your life.

You're seventeen. Your little sister Mia has frosting on her nose. Your best friend Danny is holding a lighter like it's a sword. Your grandpa is in the corner, pretending not to cry, and he's wearing the old silver watch he's been promising you for years.

"Make a wish," Mia says.

You look at the candles. You think about school, and money, and a girl named Priya who doesn't know you exist. Then you think about something else. Grandpa's hands. They shake now. They didn't used to.

So you wish for the one thing that sounds like a joke.

"I don't want anyone to run out of time."

You blow. Every flame goes out.

Nothing happens. Nothing happens for the whole night. Danny eats three slices. Mia falls asleep on the couch. Grandpa hands you the watch, and it's heavy, and warm, and still ticking. You go to bed happy.

The next morning, you step off the curb outside school, and a delivery van hits you at forty miles an hour.

You wake up on the pavement. No pain. No blood. A crowd is screaming. The driver is on his knees, crying, saying he never saw you. You stand up. Your shirt is torn. Your skin isn't. You can feel your own heartbeat, and it feels like it's laughing.

Welcome to the rule. You will never age. You will never get sick. You can't be hurt, burned, or killed. You will live forever.

Everyone else will not.

That's the whole deal. It doesn't feel like a curse. Not yet. It feels like a superpower with no instructions.

Remember the watch. It matters later.

So at first, you do what any seventeen-year-old would do. You test it. You jump off the high board with your eyes closed. You grab a hot pan from the stove. You let Danny hit you with a bat, just to see. He swings, he cries, he drops it. "Dude, you're a monster." Then he laughs so hard he has to sit on the floor. "Dude, you're a legend."

Danny is the first person you tell. He's the only one. He takes it better than you do. He writes a list on a napkin. "Things we do now that you can't die." Number one is skydiving without the parachute. Number two is a diner dare, and it's too dumb to say out loud. Number three is just a word. Everywhere.

You do all of it. Number one scares him half to death. Number two gets you banned from a chain restaurant. Number three takes ten years, one postcard at a time, and by the end you've been to forty countries, and you've still never seen him happier.

Quick pause. If you could never die, what's the first thing you'd do? Drop it below. The best comment is next week's what-if.

At thirty, you notice the first problem. It isn't you. It's Danny. He has gray in his beard. He has a mortgage. He has a daughter named Lucy. And he married Priya. Yes, that Priya, the girl who didn't know you existed. She notices you now, but only because you never look older. He introduces you as "my buddy from high school." You're still seventeen. People stare. A mom at a barbecue asks if you're lost.

So you start lying. Cousin. Nephew. Roommate. You grow a fake beard. You wear glasses you don't need. In a bank, a clerk looks at your ID, then at you, and says, "Sir, this person was born in 1998." You say, "Family resemblance."

The thing nobody tells you about living forever is how much paperwork it takes.

At forty, you can't stay anywhere for more than ten years. At fifty, you've been seventeen for thirty-three years. And at fifty-one, a woman in a lab coat knocks on your door.

Dr. Imani Okafor is sharp, polite, and impossible to scare. She works for a research lab that noticed something odd in a hospital database. A teenager with no medical history. A body that never changes. She shows you your own face in three photos, taken years apart. The face is identical.

"I don't want to take you apart," she says. "I want to understand you."

You say no. She says, "Fair." Then she asks one question nobody has ever asked you in fifty years.

"Are you happy?"

You open your mouth to say yes. Nothing comes out. She nods slowly, like that was the answer she expected. She leaves a card on the table.

You throw it in the trash. Then you take it out again, and put it in your pocket. Remember that card.

Because here's the real problem, and it's not the secret. It's the math.

The average person on Earth lives about seventy-three years. The oldest human ever recorded, Jeanne Calment of France, made it to a hundred and twenty-two. She said she once met Vincent van Gogh in her uncle's shop. Her review? "Dirty, badly dressed, and disagreeable." Even the longest life in history is a blink.

You have thousands of those blinks. And every single one has a face in it.

You start to run the numbers in your head. Mia is three years younger than you. At sixty-eight, she is a gray-haired woman who laughs exactly like she did at seven. She hugs you on the porch. Her grandkids run past, and one of them points. "Auntie, why does your brother look like a kid?"

"He's really good at skincare," Mia says.

She knows. She's always known. She never says it. She just holds your hand a little longer than she should, every time you leave.

In the year you are seventy-two, Danny's heart stops. He's seventy-one. You hold his hand in the hospital. He is small, and thin, and for the first time he looks like a stranger. His voice is paper.

"Hey," he says. "Did I finish the napkin list?"

You look at the old, folded napkin in your wallet. You've kept it for fifty-four years. Three things are crossed out. The rest are blank.

"Almost," you lie.

"Good." He smiles, eyes closed. "Don't finish it alone."

He's gone before sunrise.

At the funeral, you stand in the back row. A seventeen-year-old in a suit, at an old man's grave. His daughter Lucy looks at you for a long time. She's sixty. She has her father's eyes.

"He told me about you," she says. "I thought it was a joke."

"I wish it was."

She nods. Then she hugs you, the kind of hug that only comes from someone who inherited a secret. And she says something you'll hear in your head for the next four hundred years.

"Don't stop showing up."

By ninety, Mia is gone too. By a hundred and ten, you've outlived your whole street, your whole school, the whole block of the town where you grew up. The apartment where the cake sat is a parking lot.

This is the part of the story that the word "forever" hides. It isn't one long life. It's a long line of goodbyes with a short gap between each.

And there's a name for what happens next. It's called hedonic adaptation. Humans get used to anything. A new house, a raise, even a miracle. After a while, the brain turns it into wallpaper. You are about to find out what that looks like over three hundred years.

First, money. If you invest a single dollar at seven percent for a hundred years, you get nearly nine hundred. Wait two hundred years, and it's three-quarters of a million. You were never rich, but patience makes you a quiet billionaire. You buy a house. You buy a thousand. You buy an island. You stand on its beach with nothing to do. It's the loneliest day of your life.

Then, knowledge. You read every book. You learn forty languages, because you can. About seven thousand are spoken on Earth right now, and one of them dies, on average, every two weeks. You meet the last speaker of one. He's an old man in a village you can't pronounce. He tells you a joke in a language no one else will ever understand. You laugh. He cries. That's the end of that language.

You remember it for him. You're the only one who can.

Then, love. Maybe the worst part. And the people who love you back are never the problem. The calendar is. You love someone. You always do. Her name is Elena, then Amara, then June. They grow old. They hold your hand. They say, "It's okay." And it never is. You stop asking people their birthdays. You start counting the gray hairs instead.

In the year you are two hundred and ninety, you open your drawer and find the card. Dr. Okafor's. It's yellow. It's brittle. It's the only thing in the house older than you feel.

You call the number. It has been disconnected for two centuries. Of course it has. But you drive to the lab anyway, and you find the building, and it's a museum now. Inside is a glass case. And inside the glass case is a notebook.

Her handwriting is tiny. The last page has your name on it, and one line.

"If he's still out there, tell him it wasn't a curse. It was a job. Someone has to remember."

You hold the notebook in the dark museum for a very long time.

Then you begin.

You start walking the world with a backpack. You learn every name you can. You visit graves nobody visits. You say the name of every dead stranger out loud, and you tell their story to the next person you meet. A fisherman. A queen. A girl who sang in a choir that was never recorded. Over the years, you become something humans have never had before. An eyewitness to everything.

When someone asks what you do, you say, "I'm a historian." It's close enough.

At two hundred years old, you stand next to the oldest living tree on Earth, a pine named Methuselah, nearly forty-eight hundred years old. You press your hand against it. It's rough, and warm, and almost alive. For the first time in centuries, you feel like you've found a peer. You pick up a single seed from the ground and slip it in your pocket.

Remember that seed.

At one thousand years old, you watch the sun rise over a pyramid and realize it's still only four and a half thousand years old. Soon, you'll be older than the people who built it, by every measure that matters.

At ten thousand years, you've forgotten what your own mother's voice sounds like. You realize that the memory is the first thing to go. You have to leave notes to yourself. You carry a recorder. You keep one small box with a single silver watch inside, still ticking. Grandpa's watch. You wind it every morning. It's the only thing you still own from your first day.

At a hundred thousand years, there is no one on Earth who speaks anything you learned. The cities you saw are dust. The countries are layers of soil. Humans become something else, a new kind of people, taller, thinner, with names you can't pronounce. They treat you like a myth, and they're right. You are the oldest story they know.

At a million years, you're not even sure who you were. You only know there was a cake, and a girl who laughed, and a boy with a lighter.

You stop counting after that. Ten million years. A hundred million. Continents slide together into one giant landmass, like a puzzle finishing itself. You walk across all of it, alone, and nobody is there to tell you it's beautiful. So you tell yourself.

And then, at last, the sky begins to change.

The sun gets about one percent brighter every hundred million years. It sounds small. It isn't. In about a billion years, the oceans will boil off. In five billion, the sun swells into a red giant, and Earth is gone.

You've been walking for a very long time. And now you're the only one left to see it.

You build a ship. It takes you ten thousand years to finish it. In its hold you put a single seed vault. Millions of species. Every tree, every flower, every coral. And you put the box with the watch next to a tiny seed from an old, small pine. The one you picked up in your pocket, nine hundred million years ago.

Then you leave, and Earth shrinks behind you into a blue and white dot.

Out in the dark, nothing speaks. Nothing answers. You sit in the cockpit, and you do what you've done every night since the day Dr. Okafor's notebook found you. You say the names.

Not just Danny's. Not just Mia's. All of them. Every single person you've ever met. Every name you learned in every graveyard. Billions of lives that would otherwise be silence.

It takes you nine hours. You do it again tomorrow.

And somewhere past a star with no name, your ship drifts into a small, quiet, green world. A planet with water, and wind, and dirt. You plant that seed in the first soil you find.

A sprout comes up in a week. You press your hand against the thin green stem, and you wind the watch one last time.

Then it stops.

For the first time since the day you got it, it stops. The gears are done. After all that time, a little silver watch finally ran out of seconds.

And you laugh. You laugh until you cry. Because it's the first thing in the entire universe that you've ever seen reach the end. And somehow, it feels like a gift.

You sit under the little tree and look up. Stars as far as you can see. Somewhere out there, a candle burns for a girl you'll never meet. Somewhere, a boy is holding a lighter like a sword.

Not bad for a kid who just wanted everyone to have more time.

You lost your sister, your best friend, your whole family, and every home you ever had. You watched a language die, and you said its last joke out loud. And you learned the one thing the wish never told you. Time isn't the gift. The people you share it with are.

So tell me, would you take the deal? Would you live forever, knowing everyone else couldn't? Or would you blow out the candles, make a different wish, and let the clock keep ticking?
