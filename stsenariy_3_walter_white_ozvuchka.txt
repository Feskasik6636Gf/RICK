If you were born as Walter White, you'd walk away from a billion-dollar company for five thousand dollars, then build a bigger empire out of a beat-up RV. And the man who finally finds you is sitting at your own family dinner table.

But right now, you're a baby in New Mexico, and you're already too smart for your own good.

You grow up quiet. You love one thing more than anything else. Chemistry. While other kids are playing ball, you're staring at how a flame changes color. To you, the whole world is just matter, and matter is just a puzzle waiting for someone patient enough to solve it. You get so good at it that you win a spot at one of the best science schools in the country.

By your twenties, you're not just smart. You're one of the brightest chemists of your generation. You even help with research that ends up winning a Nobel Prize. Remember that. It matters later.

Then you do something that looks like the best move of your life. You start a company with your best friend, Elliott, and the woman you love, Gretchen. You call it Gray Matter. The three of you share a dream, a lab, and a ridiculous amount of coffee.

And then it ends.

Nobody ever tells the full story, but you leave. You walk out of your own company, and you sell your share for five thousand dollars. Five thousand. Remember that number.

Years pass. Gray Matter becomes worth billions. And the brilliant kid with the Nobel-level mind? Gone.

You're fifty now. You teach chemistry at a public high school in Albuquerque. Your students are bored. Your wallet is empty. After class, you drive to a car wash, where you wipe down someone else's cars for extra cash. A student sees you in the parking lot, and you pretend not to notice. Then he laughs, and you pretend not to hear.

At home, there's your wife Skyler, pregnant and tired. There's your son, Walter Junior, who has cerebral palsy and wants to be called Flynn. And there's a sagging old house with a bathroom light that never works.

On your fiftieth birthday, the family throws you a party. Your brother-in-law Hank, a DEA agent with a big laugh and a bigger gun, gives you a toast. He shows you pictures of a recent drug bust. A pile of cash on a table. A masked man running from a house. You nod. It's another world. It has nothing to do with you.

Then you cough.

You cough again, harder, and you almost faint at the car wash. At the hospital, a doctor looks at a scan with the face nobody wants to see. Stage three lung cancer. Inoperable. Two years, maybe.

You sit in the parking lot. You don't cry. You laugh. A long, strange, broken laugh. Because you have one question, and it isn't "why me." It's "how much money will my family have after I'm gone?"

The answer is nothing.

So the next weekend, you tag along on a ride-along with Hank. You watch his team raid a house. And through the window you see a face you recognize. A former student. A kid you once gave a failing grade. Jesse Pinkman. He jumps out of a window and runs. He's a small-time dealer who cooks bad product.

And you realize something. You're one of the best chemists alive. And no one has ever asked you to be.

You find Jesse and make him an offer. Teach him the science, and he teaches you the business. He says no. You say it's not a request. You rent an RV, and you drive into the desert wearing nothing but a respirator, your underwear, and a very straight back.

The first batch comes out pure. Not good. Pure. So pure that everyone who sees it stops talking. It's blue, and it shines like glass, and it's the most beautiful thing you've ever made.

And it ruins your life.

Because the money isn't the first problem. The first problem is a man named Emilio, and his cousin, Krazy-8. They want your product, and they want you dead. You win, barely, and you end up with a man chained in your basement and a question nobody should ever have to answer. You answer it. Remember what you did there.

Then you lie to Skyler for the first time. Not a big lie. Just a small one, a late night, a "work thing." But a small lie is the first brick of a big wall.

But here's the problem. The better you get, the more people want you. And the more they want you, the less you can step out. You stop being a teacher with a secret. You start being a criminal with a day job.

Your lawyer is a man named Saul Goodman, who has a billboard, a cheap suit, and a lot of ideas. Your fixer is a man named Mike, who never smiles. And your biggest customer is a quiet, polite, terrifying man named Gustavo Fring. He owns a fried chicken chain. He gives you a lab the size of a football field, and a salary of three million dollars for three months.

You're fifty-one. You've never made that much in your life.

Remember a name. Gus.

You tell Skyler it's from gambling. She doesn't believe you, but she lets it slide. She's got her own secrets too. And for a while, it almost works. Your son thinks you're cool. Your daughter, Holly, is born. Your cancer goes into remission. You could stop. You say that out loud. "I could stop."

You don't.

Then one night, you walk into Jesse's apartment and find his girlfriend, Jane, asleep beside him. She's in recovery, and she's pulling him back toward drugs, and she knows too much about you. Jesse is asleep. Jane rolls onto her back. And she starts to choke.

You stand there.

You could save her. You know exactly how. You put your hand out, and you stop. You watch. And she dies.

Nobody ever finds out. Not even Jesse. But her father is an air traffic controller, and the day after her funeral, he makes a mistake. Two planes collide high above the city, and wreckage rains down onto your backyard. Among it, a burned pink teddy bear lands in your pool.

Remember that bear.

You didn't push those planes. You didn't pull any trigger. But you know, deep down, what that bear really is. It's a bill. And it's yours.

By now, there's a pattern. You say it's for the family. And every time you cross a line, you say it again.

It's for the family.

Skyler finally finds out. She sits you down, and she looks at you like you're a stranger. She asks how much. You tell her. She asks what it costs. You say, I did it for us. And Skyler says the only honest thing anyone says all season. "Walt, I don't think I know who you are anymore."

That night, you say the line that people will quote for fifteen years. She tells you she's scared of what's coming. She says someone is going to come to the door. And you look her in the eye.

"I am not in danger, Skyler. I am the danger. A guy opens his door and gets shot, and you think that of me? No. I am the one who knocks."

It's the best speech of your life. It's also the moment you stop being a husband, and start being a monster who happens to live in the house.

Then you meet your match.

Gus Fring is polite. Gus Fring is patient. Gus Fring has never lost a fight, and he looks at you like a very expensive tool he's about to break. One day, he walks into a room and, with a box cutter and no expression, he kills one of his own men to prove a point. You tell yourself you've thought it through. You haven't.

So you start a war.

You use an old man in a wheelchair who hates Gus more than anyone alive. His name is Hector Salamanca. He can't talk. He can't move. All he has is a little silver bell. You plant a bomb under his chair, and you wait. When Gus walks into the room, Hector rings the bell. The room explodes. Gus walks out, straightens his tie, and collapses. Half his face is gone.

You did it. You killed the man who made you rich.

But there's still one person in the way. Jesse. He suspects you poisoned a little boy. And you did. With a plant. Lily of the valley. You make a child sick to turn Jesse back to your side. It works. Jesse hugs you. He cries. He says he's sorry for doubting you.

And you hug him back.

Then, finally, the big one. You and Jesse and Mike's crew rob a train in the desert. Thousands of gallons of the one thing you need. It works perfectly. And then a boy on a dirt bike shows up and sees you. A boy. One of your own partners shoots him. He doesn't make it.

For the first time in years, you almost throw up.

Not enough to stop, though. You make eighty million dollars. You have so much cash you can't count it. Skyler rents a storage unit. It's full. She doesn't laugh.

You say, "That's enough." For the first time, you mean it.

And then, one day, you go to the bathroom at Hank's house. You pick up a book. An old copy of Leaves of Grass, a gift from a man you worked with, who died a long time ago. You flip it open. Inside, there's a short message. "To my other favorite W.W." Not Walter White. Walt Whitman. But the handwriting is a handwriting Hank knows very well.

Hank finds it. He reads it twice. Then he walks out of the bathroom, and his face is gone white.

For the first time, your own brother-in-law looks at you like the biggest criminal in the state. He breaks your nose in your own garage. And when he finally stops, you tell him the only thing you have left. "If you don't know who I am, then maybe your best course of action is to tread lightly."

Months later, Hank is in the desert, kneeling in the sand. You've made a deal with a group of men who can protect you. They can't be bought, and they can't be stopped. They wear black, and they don't blink. You beg them to spare him. They say no. Hank looks you in the eye. He knows. He tells them, "My name is ASAC Schrader. And you can go to hell."

He's gone before you reach him.

You win the day. You lose everything else.

The money? Buried in barrels in the desert. The house? Burned, graffitied, empty. Your wife? Hiding, terrified. Your son? Shouting at you from across a kitchen. "Just die already!" Your own child. Your daughter, Holly? You take her out of the house in the middle of the night, and then you leave her at a fire station. You call Skyler on the phone and you say terrible things. You say them on purpose, because you know the police are listening. You say them so that she can walk away free.

It's the last gift you ever give your family.

And then there's nothing left. You're a thin, sick, bearded man in a cabin in the snow. You pay a stranger for one hour of company, just to have someone to talk to. You've lost almost everything. You're alone. And you still have one more thing to do.

But first, a stop in your old life. You walk into Elliott and Gretchen's house, the two people who built Gray Matter with you. You hand them a bag of money and tell them to give it to your son when he turns eighteen, as if it came from them. They don't know that two red dots on their chest are only a bluff. Five thousand dollars. That's what you sold your dream for. You just made them pay for it.

Then you go back one last time.

You walk into a room where the men who killed your brother-in-law are having dinner. Your car is parked out front. In the trunk, a machine you built yourself. You hit a button. The trunk opens. And the whole room falls.

Then, in the lab, you find Jesse, chained, thin, broken. He kills the man who held him with the very chain around his wrist. A stray bullet hits you in the side. He picks up a gun and points it at you. You tell him to do it. He says he will, but only if you say you want it. You tell him the truth. You do. And he lowers the gun. He walks away. You listen to his car roar into the night, and somewhere in the engine, a scream. A scream of pure freedom.

And, finally, for the first time in your life, you tell Skyler the truth.

"I did it for me. I liked it. I was good at it. And I was really... I was alive."

You walk through the lab one last time. You touch the steel tanks. You look at every beaker, every pipe, every little piece of equipment as if it's a person. And when you fall, you fall slowly. You land on your back, with a small, quiet smile, and a faint song drifting in from somewhere far away. It's a love song, and it's a goodbye, and it's the only music that was ever meant for you.

Not bad for a high school teacher who just wanted to leave his family enough.

You lost your brother-in-law, your partner, your house, your name, and every person who ever believed in you. You built an empire out of nothing, and left it behind in a barrel in the desert. The same mind that helped win a Nobel Prize built the most dangerous drug empire in America. You chased money, and what you found was yourself.
